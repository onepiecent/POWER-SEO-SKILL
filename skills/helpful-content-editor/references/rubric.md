# Rubric: a blog post that is helpful to readers

Based on Google's guidance on helpful, reliable, people-first content (the self-assessment questions, the Who/How/Why framework, E-E-A-T in which **trust matters most**), its guidance on writing product reviews, its guidance on AI content and its spam policies, plus editorial practice. Sources and verification levels: `printerval-blog-seo/references/sources.md`. The scale is a working tool **[Convention]**, not a Google score.

Score each dimension **0-3**: 0 = missing/wrong, 1 = weak, 2 = acceptable, 3 = outstanding. The total is 24. A post should move on to a human edit only when none of dimensions 1, 4 and 5 scores below 2.

## 1. Intent match and answering early

Ask: What is the reader trying to do when they type this keyword? Does the opening (within ~150 words) say who the post is for and what it can answer? Do the H1 and the title promise what the post actually delivers (Discover: the title states what the content is, no clickbait)?
- 3: the answer/conclusion is right at the top, with "who should read this".
- 1: the reader has to scroll past several introductory paragraphs to find the content.
- **Red flags:** an opening like "In today's fast-paced world...", an "ultimate guide" promise with thin content, a sensational title.

## 2. Original value (non-commodity)

Ask (following Google's self-assessment questions): Does the post offer original information, research or analysis? Does it describe the topic completely? Does it analyse beyond the obvious? Is there anything a reader could not get from a generic summary (production/support experience, aggregated data, real examples)?
- 3: at least one first-hand experience or original data point with a verifiable `[EXPERIENCE]`/`[DATA]`, and it changes the advice.
- 1: a rephrasing of what every top result says.
- **Red flags:** idea lists whose items are interchangeable and give no reason to choose; copying a competitor's structure.

## 3. Coverage of the reader's needs

Ask: After reading, is there an obvious question the reader still has that was not answered (size, budget, timing, drawbacks, how to personalise)? Are the secondary questions (PAA/AI Overview) inside the post? Is it organised around the reader's real situations?
- 3: anticipates and answers the next question; includes "who it is not for".
- 1: misses the basic questions.
- **Red flags:** splitting every small question into its own page (scaled-content risk), or stuffing in FAQs nobody asks.

## 4. Evidence and accuracy

Ask: Does every figure, name and date have a verifiable origin? Are there links to primary sources (prefer .gov/.gov.uk, surveys with a stated method, product-team documents)? Is there anything an AI "filled in" that sounds plausible but has not been verified (hallucination)?
- 3: every figure has a source and a date; product-specific claims match internal documents.
- 1: figures without sources; a vague "studies show".
- **Red flags:** suspiciously round figures with no source, citations that do not exist, wrong holiday dates (the US and UK differ).

## 5. Honesty and trust (Who / How / Why)

Ask: Does the reader know who wrote it and why? Is there a byline, an author page, a reviewer where needed, and real publish/update dates? Is experience claimed as "ours" real and backed by evidence (photos, measurements, notes)? Are drawbacks stated plainly? Is the commercial relationship clear?
- 3: a real author, clear sources, judgements with pros and cons, no claim beyond the evidence.
- 1: anonymous, an advertising tone, absolute claims.
- **Red flags:** "we tested" with no evidence; "customers love" with no data; self-made reviews/testimonials; delivery, environmental or health claims (run `claims-compliance-check`).

## 6. Clarity and usability

Ask: Is the structure built around the reader's questions? Short paragraphs (2-4 sentences), active sentences, simple words (US plain language / GOV.UK)? Tables and lists in the right places? Images with ALT text, accessible? Does it read well on a phone?
- 3: scannable and still understandable; no need to re-read.
- 1: a wall of text, unexplained jargon.
- **Red flags:** Flesch-Kincaid above 10 for general-audience content, repeated sentences over 30 words, long paragraphs, headings that repeat one pattern.

## 7. Natural commercial integration

Ask: **With every product removed, is the post still useful?** Does each product mention solve a specific problem for the reader at that moment? Is there any pressure to buy, a fixed price or a delivery promise?
- 3: products are mentioned as the answer ("a sharp photo will print more clearly, so choose a design like this..."), with no pushy CTA.
- 1: a list of products bolted on; repeated CTAs.
- **Red flags:** slots bunched at the end, slots in the opening, a `why` made only of slogans ("perfect gift").

## 8. Written for people, not for search engines

Ask: Do the keywords appear naturally? Are the H2s built from reader questions instead of repeating the keyword? Are several posts in the same cluster near-duplicates? Does the updated date reflect a real change?
- 3: you cannot guess the target keyword from how words are stuffed in; every post has its own purpose.
- 1: high keyword density, many H2s opening identically, posts assembled from a template.
- **Red flags:** refreshing the date without changing the content (Google advises against it); producing variant posts in bulk; "keyword-first" headings.
